The automated detection of baleen whale calls generally requires large annotated datasets, which are not available for many species and populations. We investigate whether pretrained bioacoustic embeddings, combined with a prototypical few-shot classifier, allow such calls to be detected and classified from only a handful of labelled examples. Two embedding networks are compared: Perch, a general-purpose bioacoustic model trained predominantly on birdsong, and WhaleVAD, a task-specific blue- and fin-whale call detector. Neither model is fine-tuned, and at most ten labelled examples per call type are assumed. Evaluating on Antarctic blue- and fin-whale recordings, we find that the default Perch input representation is poorly matched to low-frequency whale calls, and that increasing the frame length while restricting the mel filterbank to the frequency range occupied by the calls improves the AUC-ROC by 5.8% absolute when ten examples per call type are available. Allowing the classifier to detect each call type independently, so that segments may contain several calls, affords a further 3.3% improvement and a macro AUC-ROC of 0.739, representing a combined gain of 9.1% absolute. Both embeddings are applied to Bryde's whale vocalisations recorded in False Bay, South Africa, which neither model has encountered during pre-training. Here Perch outperforms WhaleVAD for every call type, achieving a macro AUC-ROC of 0.987 against 0.859, despite having a predominantly terrestrial training set. These results indicate that general-purpose bioacoustic embeddings, paired with an input representation matched to the calls of interest, can support whale call detection for species and populations for which large annotated datasets do not exist.
